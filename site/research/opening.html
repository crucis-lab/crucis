The opening investigation

A question from a hot apartment

Would opening the window earlier help?

Your apartment gets too warm by late morning. You want to know whether opening the window earlier tomorrow would keep it cooler.

Imagine two versions of the same morning

Open at 8 a.m.

Open at 10 a.m.

Keep the morning the same. Use the same starting room temperature, outdoor temperature, wind speed and sunlight. Change only the opening time, then compare how much heat builds up over the same period.

That’s the test we’d like to run. First, let’s look at a saved apartment simulation where we already know what affects what.

A saved apartment case

How could opening time affect the heat?

Our October 2026 study used a saved apartment case from CausalArena, a collection of simulated examples. It came with simulated measurements and a diagram of what affects what. The green arrows show one route by which opening time can affect the heat.

Read an arrow from tail to tip. Start at First opening time. The first arrow says that when you open the window affects how much it is open over the morning. Follow the next arrows through airflow to the heat that builds up.

Where the saved case came from ↗

The method we’re checking

Could a method recover those links?

In a real apartment, we might have measurements and descriptions but no diagram of what affects what. A causal-discovery method tries to infer those links from clues.

One way to form a proposal is to ask a language model, a tool that answers written questions. Give it three names from the diagram, such as opening time, how much the window is open, and airflow. Ask what could affect what.

In our study, Vashishtha and colleagues’ method asked the model about groups of three using the names and setting. It combined the replies into a diagram we call the earlier proposal. We can check it against the saved simulation rules.

Same apartment · Another clue

What can measurements add?

The earlier proposal came from the names and setting. The experiment code had also analyzed the saved measurements. The final diagram combines that analysis with the proposal for the same run.

For example, when the window opens earlier, is it usually open for more of the morning? The measurements-based procedure looks for patterns to decide what to connect. The local experiment keeps the directions that procedure picks and can use the proposal when a direction is still open.

Evidence for the intermediate comparison ↗

Back to the original decision

Would opening earlier cool the room?

The green arrows traced a route from opening time through airflow to heat. Even if the method recovered every arrow, we’d still need to measure how much warmer or cooler the room gets when we change the opening time.

For a real test, plan pairs of mornings with similar starting room temperatures and weather. Flip a coin before each pair to choose which morning gets the 8 a.m. opening. Record the starting temperature, outdoor temperature, wind and sunlight so you can check how closely the mornings match.

What the saved study tested ↗

Proposed test · Two opening times

Open at 8 a.m.

Open at 10 a.m.

Change the opening time. Keep the opening width, shade and AC settings the same. Close the window at noon on both schedules.

Record indoor temperature. Measure in the same spot every five minutes from 8 a.m. to noon on both schedules. That includes the two hours when only the earlier schedule has the window open.

Compare the averages. Average the readings for each morning and compare the two averages in each pair. Consistently lower averages for 8 a.m. would support opening earlier under those conditions.

What the apartment showed us

What can each check tell us?

Check the arrows · What we did

Did the final diagram fit the simulation?

We checked the saved final diagram against the simulation’s rules. The arrow between the window boxes pointed the opposite way. That gives us a specific conflict to report for this run.

Test the decision · What we proposed

Would opening at 8 keep the room cooler?

We’d try 8 and 10 on comparable mornings and compare average room temperature from 8 a.m. to noon. The difference would estimate how much warmer or cooler the room got when we opened earlier under those conditions.

Why we’re building Crucis

A reader should be able to find the exact diagram behind a claim about arrows or the readings behind a claim about cooling. Crucis is a framework for auditing causal-discovery systems. It needs to keep what the system returned, run checks that match each claim, and link each finding to the evidence behind it.

The audit needs testing too. If we deliberately give it the earlier proposal labeled as the final diagram, can it catch the mix-up?

Research basis and scope ↗

The apartment showed two different jobs: checking an explanation and testing a decision. To make either finding useful, we must also choose the right answer, use an appropriate reference and let a reader retrace the check.
